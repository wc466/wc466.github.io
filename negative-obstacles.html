the problem

Long-range negative-obstacle detection

Trenches and pits are the most dangerous obstacles for an off-road vehicle, and the hardest to see. The leading edge hides most of the hole, so a LiDAR gets almost no points from inside it. In my tests the pits only became visible in raw point clouds about 3 m away, which left our John Deere R-Gator about 2 m to react. The sections below explain why that happens, how I enhanced the data, and the detector that pushed detection out to 8.5 m.

step 1 — why pits hide

The geometry of seeing (and not seeing) obstacles

From a LiDAR scan-line model, the deepest part of the back wall that any beam can reach is d = H·W / R: proportional to sensor height and pit width, inversely proportional to range. If d falls below the terrain noise, no algorithm can detect the pit. This sets the detector's threshold and the best range we can hope for. Drag the sliders to try it.

Positive obstacles fade too: a traffic cone at 25 m → 9 m

From a 90°-FOV drive toward a 28-inch (0.71 m) cone: ground removed with Patchwork, non-ground points clustered (DBSCAN), and the cone tracked frame to frame. Its point count follows the angular-resolution model n ≈ A / (R² · Δθv · Δθh), so it falls as 1/R².

step 2 — seeing more with the same sensor

Multi-frame fusion with ICP + pose-graph optimization

Ground resolution falls with range, so I fuse a sliding window of 5 scans. Each pair is aligned with coarse-to-fine point-to-plane ICP. Consecutive pairs become odometry edges and the rest become loop-closure edges, and a Levenberg–Marquardt pose graph solves for consistent poses. The trick that made it fast: register on 1 m voxel-downsampled clouds, then apply the transforms to the full-resolution scans.

step 3 — the detector

From fused points to a 5 cm cost map

The detector turns each fused cloud into a bird's-eye cost map by looking for the two signatures the geometry predicts: the elevation drop on the pit's back wall, and the empty, occluded cells right in front of it. Step through the stages, or play the drive as the vehicle approaches the pits.

1 · Elevation disparity

Mean height per 5 cm cell minus a terrain trend from a coarse grid with bicubic interpolation. Cells that drop more than the threshold back-wall depth are low-elevation regions. This tolerates slopes but catches sharp dips.

2 · Occluded regions

For each low-elevation cell, look up to 40 cm back toward the sensor. Empty cells in the density map there are the shadow the leading edge casts. The two together mark the obstacle. This search is vectorised in NumPy.

3 · Clean-up & GPU

A 2×2 erosion drops isolated cells, then 2 dilation passes add a safety margin. After line-profiling, the nearest-neighbour hole filling moved to a Numba CUDA kernel.

The interactive demo re-runs the research code as committed (12 cm disparity threshold, 2 m coarse grid); the project evaluation used 0.15 m and 1.5 m.

results

Pits detected at 8.5 m instead of ~3 m

Over 50 consecutive fused frames (5 s of driving), the detector produced a cost map in about 0.5 s per frame on a laptop. The larger pits appear first and the smallest last, as the geometry predicts. The model gives 8.9 m for the 0.7 m pit, and the field result was ~8.5 m.
